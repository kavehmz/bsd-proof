## 2. One geometric lattice difference

At z=x+iy let
$$\Lambda_z=\mathbb Zz+\mathbb Z,\qquad
\Lambda'_z=\mathbb Zz+N^{-1}\mathbb Z,$$
and use the SAME norm $|w|^2/y$ on both. Define, for u>0,
$$\Theta(z,u)=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb Z}
             \exp\left(-\pi u\frac{|mz+n|^2}{y}\right),$$
$$\Theta'(z,u)=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb Z}
             \exp\left(-\pi u\frac{|mz+n/N|^2}{y}\right)
              =\Theta(Nz,u/N),$$
$$H_N(z,u)=\Theta'(z,u)-\Theta(z,u). \tag{2.1}$$
These are full convergent lattice sums. The inclusion $\Lambda_z\subset
\Lambda'_z$ is the universal cyclic isogeny with kernel generated by
1/N in $\mathbb C/\Lambda_z$. The lattice data exist integrally on the
modular stack; on the fine cover they are the Betti homology lattices of
the universal elliptic curves. The norm is the transported source
Hodge norm: on $\Lambda'_z$, of covolume y/N, it is 1/N times the
target's intrinsic principal normalized norm. This is why the same
denominator y, rather than y/N, is used in both sums.
The central automorphism -1 permutes summands, so the scalar kernel
descends to the effective orbifold.

**[NEW] Proposition 2.1 (invariance, duality and exact Mellin kernel).**
Both kernels in (2.1) are invariant under $\Gamma_0(N)$. One has
$$\Theta(z,u)=u^{-1}\Theta(z,u^{-1}),\qquad
\Theta'(z,u)=\frac N u\Theta(Nz,N/u), \tag{2.2}$$
and, for $\Re s>1$,
$$\boxed{\mathcal E_N(z,s)=\frac{\Gamma(s)}2
              \int_0^\infty u^{s-1}H_N(z,u)\,du.} \tag{2.3}$$

*Proof.* For $\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
$c\equiv0\pmod N$, multiplication by $cz+d$ identifies the first
lattice at $\gamma z$ with the first lattice at z, preserving the stated
normalized norm. For the second lattice the coordinate transformation is
$$(m,n)\longmapsto (am+cn/N,\;Nbm+dn),$$
an integral determinant-one bijection. This proves invariance.

The quadratic form $Q_z(m,n)=|mz+n|^2/y$ has determinant one.
If J is the standard integral alternating matrix, its inverse matrix is
$J^{\mathsf T}Q_zJ$. Gaussian Fourier transformation and Poisson
summation therefore give the first identity (2.2). Applying it to
$\Theta(Nz,u/N)$ gives the second, including the factor N/u.

Let $E(z,s)$ be the level-one Eisenstein series with incoming term
$y^s$, and $E^*(z,s)=\xi(2s)E(z,s)$. Termwise gamma integration gives
$$\frac12\int_0^\infty u^{s-1}(\Theta(z,u)-1)du
 =\frac{\Gamma(s)}{2\pi^s}
       \sum_{(m,n)\ne(0,0)}\frac{y^s}{|mz+n|^{2s}}
 =E^*(z,s). \tag{2.4}$$
The factor two comes from primitive pairs modulo sign; their positive
common divisors contribute $\zeta(2s)$. The u/N substitution in the
second lattice gives $N^sE^*(Nz,s)$. Thus the right side of (2.3) is
$\Gamma(s)(N^sE^*(Nz,s)-E^*(z,s))$, which equals its left side by the
proved oldform identity
$E_\infty=(N^sE(Nz,s)-E(z,s))/(N^{2s}-1)$. $\square$

This proof uses ordinary Gaussian Poisson summation, including its
determinant factor, and direct Mellin integration; it does not import an
uncomputed theta-lift normalization. The classical Rankin–Selberg and
Eisenstein framework is given in Zagier, *The Rankin–Selberg method for
automorphic functions which are not of rapid decay*, J. Fac. Sci. Tokyo
28 (1982), 415–438 ([author copy](https://people.mpim-bonn.mpg.de/zagier/files/rankin-selberg/fulltext.pdf)).
All identities needed here are proved explicitly above or in the reviewed
unfolding, so no regularization convention is inferred from that reference.

## 3. Both cusp boundaries and both heat ends

Let $\sigma_0z=-1/(Nz)$. Level-one invariance gives
$$\Theta(\sigma_0z,u)=\Theta(Nz,u),\qquad
\Theta(N\sigma_0z,u)=\Theta(z,u). \tag{3.1}$$
The proved Fricke relations $f|_2W_N=-f$ and $g|_2W_N=-g$ imply
$F(\sigma_0z)=F(z)$. In either width-one cusp coordinate,
$|F|\le C y^2e^{-cy}$ for y sufficiently large. This controls both
cusps; a level-one fundamental region alone would not do so.

For clarity, (2.3) has the following FULL constant terms, in those two
coordinates:
$$\begin{aligned}
\mathcal E_{N,\infty}^{(0)}(z,s)&=
 \Gamma(s)\xi(2s)(N^{2s}-1)y^s
 +(N-1)\Gamma(s)\xi(2s-1)y^{1-s},\\
\mathcal E_{N,0}^{(0)}(z,s)&=
 \Gamma(s)\xi(2s-1)(N^s-N^{1-s})y^{1-s}.
\end{aligned} \tag{3.2}$$
They follow by substituting the level-one constant term in (2.3), or
by Poisson summation in n before Mellin integration. Their residue is
$(N-1)/2$. In particular the pole coefficient and all its derivatives
have not been lost by writing a heat integral.

**[NEW] Lemma 3.1 (joint endpoint control).** For $a=1,N$,
$$\int_Y|F(z)|(\Theta(az,u)-1)d\mu
       =O(e^{-c_a\sqrt u})\quad(u\longrightarrow\infty), \tag{3.3}$$
after decreasing $c_a>0$ if necessary. After subtraction of its
explicit small-u term, $H_N$ satisfies the analogous estimate
$$\int_Y|F(z)|\left|H_N(z,u)-\frac{N-1}{u}\right|d\mu
       =O(u^{-1}e^{-c/\sqrt u})\quad(u\longrightarrow0). \tag{3.4}$$
Every fixed number of derivatives admits the same estimates with
additional powers of u, $u^{-1}$ and cusp height.

*Proof.* On a compact part of Y the least eigenvalue of $Q_z$ is
bounded below. On each cusp strip $|x|\le1/2$, y large, the least
eigenvalues of the forms for z and Nz are at least $c_N/y$.
Formula (3.1) gives these same two forms at the other cusp. Hence the
nonzero lattice sums are bounded by a constant times
$\sum_{(m,n)\ne0}e^{-c(u/y)(m^2+n^2)}$. This sum is
$O((1+y/u)e^{-c'u/y})$, with a smaller positive constant $c'$.
Multiplication by the cusp bound for $|F|d\mu$ reduces the estimate
to integrals of a polynomial times $e^{-cy-c'u/y}dy$. Split at
$y=\sqrt u$, or use the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality after
retaining half the factor $e^{-cy}$; the result is (3.3). Polynomial
factors can be absorbed by decreasing the exponent constant.

By (2.2), exactly
$$H_N(z,u)-\frac{N-1}{u}
 =\frac N u(\Theta(Nz,N/u)-1)
       -\frac1u(\Theta(z,1/u)-1). \tag{3.5}$$
Now apply (3.3), with its constant rescalings of u, to obtain (3.4).
Differentiating a Gaussian introduces only polynomial factors in the
coordinates, u and lattice entries. Absorb the lattice factors into
half its Gaussian exponent and repeat the same estimates. This proves
the derivative assertion. $\square$

These estimates also justify exchanging the z and u integrations for
$\Re s>1$ in (2.3). At zero its sole nondecaying joint term is
$(N-1)/u$; at infinity (3.3) applies. They justify all logarithmic
derivatives after that term is subtracted.

## 4. The paired heat trace and its exact completed Mellin transform

Define the single-level trace
$$T_F(u)=\int_YF(z)(\Theta(z,u)-1)d\mu
       =\int_YF(z)\Theta(z,u)d\mu.$$
The second equality uses $\int F=0$, not deletion of the zero vector
in an unpaired formula. By Fricke invariance and (3.1),
$$K_F(u):=\int_YF(z)H_N(z,u)d\mu=T_F(u/N)-T_F(u). \tag{4.1}$$
Poisson summation, again using $\int F=0$, gives
$$T_F(u)=u^{-1}T_F(1/u). \tag{4.2}$$
Lemma 3.1 shows rapid decay at infinity; (4.2) then gives rapid decay
at zero. Therefore
$$M_T(s)=\int_0^\infty u^{s-1}T_F(u)du$$
is entire, and $M_T(s)=M_T(1-s)$.

**[NEW] Proposition 4.1 (a global product identity with no suppressed
Euler factor).** With
$$\Lambda(E,s)=N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(E,s),$$
one has
$$\boxed{M_T(s)=-\frac{24}{\sqrt N}
                     \Lambda(E,s)\Lambda(E,s+1).} \tag{4.3}$$
In particular $M_T(1)=M_T'(1)=0$, and
$$\boxed{\mathcal M=\frac14\int_0^\infty(\log u)^2K_F(u)du
       =\frac{N-1}{4}\int_0^\infty(\log u)^2T_F(u)du.} \tag{4.4}$$

*Proof.* In the domain of absolute convergence, (2.3) and (4.1) give
$$P(s):=\int_YF\mathcal E_N(z,s)d\mu
       =\frac{\Gamma(s)}2(N^s-1)M_T(s). \tag{4.5}$$
The reviewed complete unfolding states
$$\int_YFE_\infty(z,s)d\mu
 =-\frac{24\Gamma(s+1)}{(4\pi)^{s+1}}
       \frac{L(E,s+1)L(E,s)}{\zeta(2s)(1+N^{-s})}.$$
Its split multiplicative Euler factor at N is essential. Multiplying
by $\Gamma(s)\xi(2s)(N^{2s}-1)$ and comparing with (4.5) gives
$$M_T(s)=-\frac{12N^s\Gamma(s)\Gamma(s+1)}
                    {4^s\pi^{2s+1}}L(E,s)L(E,s+1),$$
which is (4.3). Equality continues from that nonempty half-plane to
the whole plane. No division at a zero of $N^s-1$ is required in this
continuation argument.

The exact order two at s=1 and $L(E,2)\ne0$ give the two lower
moments. Write s=1+t in (4.5). Since the constant and linear moments
vanish, the second coefficient is
$\frac14\int(\log u)^2K_F(u)du$; derivatives of $\Gamma(1+t)$
multiply only the vanished moments. Changing variables u=Nw in (4.1)
and using those same two moments proves the second equality (4.4).
Finally the coefficient of t² in (4.3) is
$-3N\ell L(E,2)/\pi^3$. Multiplication by $(N-1)/2$ in (4.5)
gives exactly (1.2). $\square$

Thus the entire modular sum, rather than a finite set of its kernels,
has one canonical analytic Mellin source. The product in (4.3) is an
identity of completed L-functions, not an assertion that their central
leading term has already been identified with a motivic determinant.

## 8. An exact determinant comparison target after that division
